Kitchen garden handbook

A small vegetable garden, from the first plan to the last jar on the shelf. Each part stands on its own: read it in order, or go straight to the job at hand.

Planning

A little thought before the first spade saves a season of fixing mistakes.

Choosing a spot

Most vegetables want at least six hours of direct sun. Watch the plot for a day and note where shade falls in the morning and the evening.

Pick a place close to a tap and away from big trees, whose roots take water and food long before your crops do.

Bed sizes

Beds about 1.2 m wide let you reach the middle from either side, so you never step on the soil. Paths of 40 cm leave room to kneel and to push a barrow.

What to grow

Grow what you like to eat. For a first year, pick crops that forgive mistakes:

  • radishes, ready in four weeks;
  • lettuce and other salad leaves, picked a few leaves at a time;
  • dwarf beans, which need no poles;
  • courgettes: one or two plants feed a family.

Keeping notes

Write down what you sowed, where and when, and how it did. By the third year the notebook knows the garden better than anyone.

Soil

Good soil grows good plants. Feed the soil and it feeds the crops.

Knowing your soil

Squeeze a damp handful. Sandy soil falls apart, clay holds its shape like putty, and loam, the gardener's favourite, holds together and then crumbles at a touch.

Compost

Spread about 5 cm of compost on the beds each year. A heap needs both kinds of waste:

  • greens: kitchen peelings, grass clippings, young weeds;
  • browns: dry leaves, straw, torn cardboard.

Roughly equal amounts of each, turned now and then, make compost in six months to a year.

Digging or not

Digging breaks up the soil's own structure and brings weed seeds to the light. Many growers now lay compost on top and let worms carry it down: this is the no-dig way.

Testing the pH

Most vegetables like a pH between 6 and 7. A cheap kit from a garden shop tells you where you stand. Garden lime raises the pH; sulfur lowers it, slowly.

Sowing

Seeds carry everything a plant needs to start. Your job is to give them the right depth, warmth and water.

Reading a seed packet

The packet gives the depth, the spacing between plants and rows, and the months to sow. Seeds lose strength with age: a packet kept dry and cool lasts two or three years.

Sowing depth

A rule of thumb: sow a seed twice as deep as it is wide. Tiny seeds such as lettuce go just under the surface; beans go in about 5 cm.

Starting indoors

Tomatoes, peppers and squash need a long warm season. Sow them on a bright windowsill six to eight weeks before the last frost, and move them out once nights stay mild.

Harden them off first: a week of short days outside, longer each day, before they stay out for good.

Thinning

Seedlings come up crowded. Pull the weakest ones while they are small, leaving the strongest at the spacing on the packet. Thinned salad seedlings are good to eat.

Watering

Water matters most when plants are young and when fruit is swelling.

How much

Most beds need about 2.5 cm of water a week, rain included. Water deeply and less often: roots follow the water down and the plants cope better with dry spells.

When to water

Water in the morning. Leaves dry off in the sun, and fewer slugs come out than on a damp evening.

Mulch

A layer of straw, dry leaves or grass clippings keeps the soil moist and the weeds down. Keep it a finger's width away from stems.

Collecting rain

A barrel under a downpipe fills fast in a storm. Rainwater is soft and the right temperature for plants. Keep a lid on it.

Pests

A few holes in a leaf are normal. Act when a pest starts to win.

Slugs and snails

They feed at night and love young seedlings.

By hand

Go out with a torch after dark, pick them off, and move them far away. Two or three evenings make a real difference.

Barriers

Rings of copper tape around pots, or a band of grit around a bed, keep many of them off.

Aphids

Small green or black insects bunch on shoot tips. A hard spray of water knocks them off; ladybirds and their larvae eat them by the hundred.

Birds

Pigeons strip cabbages and blackbirds take berries. Fine netting on hoops keeps both out. Pull the net tight so no bird gets tangled.

Friendly helpers

Flowers such as marigolds and poached-egg plants bring hoverflies, whose young eat aphids. A small pond brings frogs, which eat slugs.

Harvest

The best part. Picking at the right time makes the crop taste its best.

When it is ready

Most crops taste best young. Pick beans before the seeds bulge, courgettes at 15 cm, and carrots once the tops of the roots show a good colour.

Picking often

Beans, peas and courgettes make more when picked. Leave old pods on the plant and it stops flowering.

Time of day

Pick leafy crops in the cool morning, when they are full of water. They stay crisp for days in the fridge.

Gluts

Everything ripens at once. Share with neighbours, freeze, or pickle. Next year, sow a little every two weeks instead of all on one day.

Storage

A good store stretches the harvest into winter.

Cool and dark

Potatoes keep for months in paper sacks in a cool, dark, frost-free shed. Light turns them green; green potatoes should not be eaten.

Drying

Onions and garlic dry in the sun for a week, then hang in a dry place. Herbs dry in bunches, upside down, out of the sun.

Freezing

Blanch beans and peas in boiling water for two minutes, cool them fast in cold water, then freeze them flat in bags so they do not stick together.

Root clamps

An old way to store carrots and beetroot outside: a heap on straw, covered with more straw and then soil, with a tuft of straw at the top to let air in.

Calendar

A rough year for a temperate garden. Move each job a few weeks to match your own frosts.

Late winter

Plan the beds, order seeds, and start onions and early tomatoes indoors. Spread compost when the soil is not frozen.

Spring

Sow hardy crops outside: peas, broad beans, radishes, salad. Wait for the last frost before beans, squash and tomatoes go out.

Summer

Water, weed and pick. Sow quick crops such as radishes and salad into gaps as earlier crops finish.

Autumn

Clear spent plants to the compost heap, cover bare soil with leaves, and plant garlic for next summer.
